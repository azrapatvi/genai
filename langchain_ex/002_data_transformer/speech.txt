The Clockmaker of Ratnapur

In a small hill town called Ratnapur, there lived an old clockmaker named Dinkar Rao. His shop stood at the end of Lantern Street, right next to the town's only bakery. The shop was tiny, but it was packed with hundreds of clocks: wall clocks, pocket watches, cuckoo clocks, and even a giant grandfather clock that had not worked for forty years.

Everyone in Ratnapur knew Dinkar. He was seventy-two years old, wore round glasses, and always had a pencil behind his ear. Children loved him because he gave them small brass gears as gifts. Adults respected him because he could fix any clock in the world. But there was one clock he never touched, the grandfather clock in the corner of his shop. When people asked why, he would just smile and say, "That one is waiting for the right time."

One rainy morning in July, a girl named Meera walked into the shop. She was twelve years old, soaked from head to toe, and carrying a broken silver pocket watch wrapped in a handkerchief. "My grandfather left me this," she said. "The doctor in the city said it cannot be repaired. Can you try?"

Dinkar took the watch and turned it over slowly. On the back, there was a tiny engraving of a mountain and a river. His hands started to shake. "Where did your grandfather get this?" he asked. Meera said she did not know. He had died last winter, and this watch was the only thing he left behind, along with a note that said: "Take it to Ratnapur."

Dinkar did not sleep that night. He opened the watch under his lamp and found something strange inside. Behind the main gear, there was a folded piece of paper as thin as an onion skin. On it was a drawing of the grandfather clock in his shop, and under the drawing, three numbers: 7, 21, 1974.

The next morning, Dinkar told Meera a story he had never told anyone. Fifty years ago, when he was a young man, he had a best friend named Harish. They were both clockmakers, and they had a dream of building the most accurate clock in India. They worked together for three years in this very shop. On the night of July 21, 1974, they finally finished the grandfather clock. But that same night, they had a terrible argument. Harish wanted to take the clock to Mumbai and sell it to a rich collector. Dinkar wanted to keep it in Ratnapur for the people of the town. The argument became so loud that neighbors woke up. In the end, Harish walked out in the rain and never came back.

"I always thought he was angry with me," Dinkar said quietly. "I stopped the clock that night, and I never started it again. I promised myself I would only wind it when Harish returned."

Meera looked at the numbers on the paper. "Seven, twenty-one, nineteen seventy-four. It is the date you finished the clock. But why would my grandfather hide it inside a watch?"

They both looked at the grandfather clock. Dinkar walked over, opened the glass door of the pendulum case, and set the clock's hands to 7:21. Then he heard a soft click. A small wooden panel at the bottom of the clock slid open. Inside, there was a leather pouch and a letter.

The letter was written in Harish's handwriting. It said: "Dear Dinkar, I am sorry for that night. You were right. The clock belongs to Ratnapur. I was too proud to say it, so I left. I have spent my whole life in Pune, making clocks for strangers, but I never made one as good as ours. When I am gone, my granddaughter will bring you my watch. Please forgive me, my friend. Wind the clock, and let it tell the time again."

In the leather pouch, there was a small brass key, the exact key needed to wind the grandfather clock. Harish had taken it that night fifty years ago, without even realizing it was in his pocket. For half a century, he had kept it safe, hoping to return it one day.

Dinkar sat down on his old wooden stool and cried. Meera sat next to him and held his hand. After a while, he stood up, wiped his eyes, and put the brass key into the clock. He turned it twelve times. The pendulum started to swing, slowly at first, and then steadily. Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock. For the first time in fifty years, the grandfather clock of Ratnapur was alive again.

By evening, the whole town had gathered outside the shop. The bakery owner brought fresh bread. The schoolteacher brought her students. Someone even brought a small drum. When the clock struck six, its deep bell echoed through every street of Ratnapur, and people cheered as if it were a festival.

Dinkar fixed Harish's silver pocket watch that same week. He gave it back to Meera, and it worked perfectly. He also made her an offer. "If you want to learn," he said, "you can come to my shop every day after school. I will teach you everything about clocks."

Meera agreed happily. Over the next years, she became the best young clockmaker in the region. When Dinkar finally retired at the age of eighty, he handed over the shop to her. She kept the name on the board exactly as it was, "Rao and Harish, Clockmakers," so that the two old friends would always be together.

To this day, the grandfather clock still stands in the corner of the shop on Lantern Street in Ratnapur. It has never stopped again. And if you visit at exactly 7:21 in the evening, people say you can hear the pendulum tick a little louder, as if two old friends are laughing together somewhere.
